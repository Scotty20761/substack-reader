Executive Summary

  1. A thermal design is now in place: chips run hotter, and each satellite carries one NVL72 rack. SpaceX has disclosed AI1 at 250 kW peak and 175 kW average with a 160 m² radiator, but not the operating temperature. Working backward from our June radiator table, that implies a radiator surface of ~65–75°C and chip junction temperatures of 80–90°C. Our industry checks corroborate this: the space version runs its chips at junction temperatures of 80–90°C. That is feasible from an engineering standpoint, since chips in terrestrial racks often run above 80°C at full load, and the temperature itself is not a problem for the chips; the binding constraint is memory. In orbit, running hot is worth far more for heat rejection: in a vacuum, heat can only be shed by radiation, and radiative capacity rises with the fourth power of temperature. Rejecting heat at ~40°C, as is customary on the ground, would require 240–290 m² of radiator; by running hotter, a ~2 t satellite carries exactly one NVL72, a Starship carries ~40 satellites, and no on-orbit assembly is needed.
  1. The compute payload is locked: a space version of NVL72, codename Kessel. In August, SpaceX announced it is co-designing AI1’s compute payload with NVIDIA, using Vera CPUs and Rubin GPUs. the space rack architecture was finalized in October: it keeps the 72-GPU NVLink domain, compresses the 48U upright terrestrial rack into a ~9U flat enclosure, and cuts the weight to a few hundred kilograms. The first generation targets inference; large-model training stays on the ground. The chips and HBM are standard parts. Radiation is addressed through an external shield, HBM’s built-in ECC and system-level fault tolerance, and heat through a single liquid loop running straight to the external radiator, concentrating demand for advanced materials and lightweight alloys in the shielding, radiator and structural components.
  1. Supply-chain orders are in: 6,000 racks for 2027, equal to 1.05 GW. SpaceX has ordered 6,000 space-specific racks for 2027 from a leading server ODM, the sole JDM (joint design manufacturer) on the program, which is due to complete NPI in May 2027, in time for the test satellite, and finish production in December. The 6,000 racks equate to 432,000 GPUs and ~$42–45 billion of hardware value at terrestrial rack pricing; at one rack per satellite, they equal 1.05 GW. Lifetime volume for the program is ~30,000 racks, matching the plan for a first phase of ~30,000 satellites, or ~2.16 million GPUs at 72 per rack, equal to 5.25 GW (7.5 GW peak); the first 6,000 racks are a fifth of that.
  1. Constraints on the ground are pushing compute into orbit. Democrats won both houses of Congress in the November 3 midterms, and Texas’s incoming governor has pledged to pause new data centers until the legislature sets rules. Permitting, grid-connection and cost-allocation pressure on terrestrial data centers is rising, and the impact falls mainly on capacity added from 2028, just as Starmind scales. Orbital compute needs no land, grid connection or water, and no state or local data-center permits. As constraints on terrestrial capacity tighten, orbital compute becomes more attractive.
  1. Bull case: ~1 GW on orbit by end-2028. In our bull case, Starship has ~220 launches available in 2028 (Starbase expanded to 100, plus 44 from LC-39A and 76 from SLC-37), of which ~70%, or ~154, go to compute satellites. At 40 satellites per launch, that is enough to launch all 6,000 racks, so we estimate end-2028 on-orbit compute of ~1 GW in our bull case (average computepayload power at 175 kW per satellite).
  1. Two checkpoints ahead. Flight 15, expected no earlier than late November, will attempt to catch both stages with the tower; a successful catch and reflight are what support 30–40 launches in 2027. The test satellite launching on Starship in May 2027 will deliver the first on-orbit thermal data and show whether the 175 kW average per satellite is achievable in orbit, which is what our GW estimates rest on.

Five Months After the SPCX IPO

Subscribed

It has been nearly five months since SPCX’s June IPO. At the time, we treated space compute as an option embedded in the valuation, and our view rested on three calls. First, the real bottleneck is heat rejection. Second, GW-scale monolithic structures are not feasible; what works is small units in a large constellation.

Third, on timing: company filings pointed to deployment as early as 2028, we treated Musk’s launch ramp as a long-dated narrative, and the checkpoints were Starship’s cadence in 1H27 and whether the first AI satellite reaches orbit in 2H27. Over the past five months, four links in the chain from satellite design to launch have made real progress: the satellite design, the compute payload, supply-chain orders and the launch schedule. One link still awaits validation by 2027 test satellites: on-orbit thermal data.

Of our three calls, the first two are consistent with SpaceX’s current design direction: a hotter radiator, one rack per satellite and formation flying in clusters of ~10. The third is moving faster than we expected in June, as test satellites could fly as early as 1H27.

the rack architecture for the space version of NVL72 has been finalized. A leading server ODM is due to complete server NPI in May 2027, in time for the window when Starship begins launching test satellites, and to finish production of 6,000 racks by December. The Satellite Design Is Locked

When SpaceX filed with the FCC on January 30 for up to 1 million “orbital data center” satellites, it had not shown what the satellite would look like. On June 8, Musk unveiled the first-generation satellite, AI1, in a technical update: a compute payload of 150 kW peak and 120 kW average, 70 kW per ton, a 110 m² double-sided deployable liquid-cooled radiator rejecting ~1,400 W/m², an operating altitude of 600–800 km, and ~1 Tbps inter-satellite laser links. The same month, an xAI trademark filing confirmed the project name, Starmind.

On July 16, Musk raised peak power to ~250 kW (battery-assisted) and average power to ~160 kW, on the grounds that a satellite of that size fits exactly one NVIDIA Rubin NVL72 rack. A supplemental FCC filing in September set a persatellite mass cap of 4,000 kg, roughly seven times the mass of a Starlink V2 Mini. The website now lists 250 kW peak and 175 kW average, a 75 m wingspan, 30 m deployed height, a 160 m² liquid-cooled radiator and a sun-synchronous orbit.

On October 6, SpaceX released a formation-flying video and a space-safety page: tight clusters of ~10 satellites, ~10 Tbps of intra-cluster bandwidth, about half the fleet in sun-synchronous orbit (SSO), a 5-year design life followed by disposal into a heliocentric graveyard orbit, and thousands of satellites deploying as early as late 2027. the latest (October) design has ~800 m² of deployed solar arrays supplying ~210 kW, with batteries covering the gap to the 250 kW peak.

The satellite weighs no more than 2 t, about half the FCC cap; most of the mass sits in the solar arrays and radiator, and the rack itself is squeezed down to a few hundred kilograms. At 100 t (metric tons) of payload capacity, and including deployment hardware, one Starship carries ~40 satellites.

Our June thesis was that the true bottleneck for orbital data centers is heat rejection. In a vacuum, heat can only be shed by radiation: rejecting 1 MW of waste heat takes 480–850 m² of radiator at 50–100°C, and a 1 GW monolith would need 0.5–0.85 km², so GW-scale single structures are not feasible. Raising the operating temperature trades area against the fourth power of temperature, but chip temperature limits cap how far that can go.

SpaceX has not disclosed AI1’s radiator operating temperature, but it has disclosed area and power: 160 m² for 250 kW, or 0.64 m² per kW. Using the double-sided radiation and 0.95 emissivity assumptions applied in June, 1 MW at 75°C requires 634 m², or 0.63 m² per kW, so AI1 falls right in that column. The June design’s 110 m² for 150 kW works out to 0.73 m² per kW, or ~65°C, consistent with SpaceX’s own 1,400 W/m² figure.

With the radiator surface at 65–75°C, adding the temperature drop across the cold plates and fluid loop puts peak chip junction temperatures at 80–90°C. On the ground, by comparison, liquid-cooled racks typically run coolant at 25–45°C, the heat is ultimately handed to cooling towers via convection and evaporation, and junction temperatures at full load are 60–85°C. AI1 shifts the entire thermal chain upward.

On our assumptions, coolant leaves the cold plates at ~70–80°C and returns from the radiator at 60–70°C; the radiator surface averages 65–75°C, and the chips run at the upper end of their spec. Excluding external heat loads, raising the radiator surface from 40°C to 75°C cuts the required area by a third; raising it to 90°C cuts it by nearly half.

The area saved is what makes the form factor work. AI1’s radiator is a blade-like panel sandwiched between two solar wings and held edge-on to the sun; the solar arrays, at ~800 m² deployed, are the largest deployable structure on the satellite. Rejecting heat at ~40°C, as is customary on the ground, would require 240–290 m² of radiator, and stowed volume, deployment mechanisms, micrometeoroid exposure and fluid-loop length would all scale up with it.

Starship might no longer carry dozens per launch, and on-orbit assembly might become unavoidable. That is where “one rack per satellite” comes from. We crosschecked this against rack power. With chips at 80–90°C and the radiator surface averaging 65–75°C, 160 m² (single-side panel area, radiating from both sides, emissivity 0.95, excluding external heat input from the sun and Earth IR) can reject ~225–250 kW.

A terrestrial Vera Rubin NVL72 rack draws ~220 kW, of which the 72 Rubin GPUs account for 130–166 kW (1.8–2.3 kW each); the space version, without BlueField and other peripherals, will draw slightly less. This heat budget covers the rack only; the satellite bus and laser terminals are budgeted separately.

The numbers line up: one satellite carries exactly one NVL72, consistent with Musk’s rationale for raising power in July. the space version is designed to run in this temperature range, which is not a problem for the chips themselves; the binding constraint is memory. During eclipse, the coolant loop also has to be kept warm to prevent freezing. On paper, one rack per satellite means each satellite fits in the fairing, deploys on its own and needs no assembly in orbit.

The real heat balance still has to account for solar and Earth IR loads and shadowing, and the 1H27 test satellite will provide the first on-orbit data.

One rack per satellite comes at a cost, mainly in three areas. The first is depreciation. On the ground, only GPUs and servers are depreciated over five years; buildings, power and cooling are 15–25-year assets that can be reused across three or four GPU generations, and retired GPUs can be redeployed to inference. On AI1, the solar arrays, radiator, structure and propulsion retire with the GPUs after five years, so every refresh means launching a new satellite, and non-compute capex per kW has to be recovered within five years.

Google’s paper estimates that once launch prices fall to ~$200/kg (which the paper expects by the mid-2030s), launch cost amortized over satellite life is roughly on par with a terrestrial data center’s power bill; the paper also notes that this is not a complete economic analysis. The second is serviceability. Large terrestrial clusters see annualized GPU failure rates in the low single digits, and failed parts can be swapped; in orbit they cannot, and planning assumes a 10–20% cumulative loss of compute over five years.

To protect a five-year life, terrestrial operators keep junction temperatures below 85°C, whereas AI1 runs at 80–90°C. Most chip failure mechanisms are temperaturedriven; by the common rule of thumb every 10°C increase roughly halves life, though the actual acceleration depends on the failure mechanism.

The half of the fleet not in SSO also passes in and out of eclipse every 95 minutes, or about 5,500 thermal cycles a year; actual wear depends on how far device temperatures swing, but solder joints and connectors face additional thermal-fatigue risk from orbital day-night cycles. The third is margin. Ground cooling has redundancy; AI1’s radiator area is fixed, so coating degradation, micrometeoroid strikes and 2–3% annual solar-array degradation all have to be absorbed by throttling power.

Together, these three factors will slow the path to positive unit economics. The two inputs with the largest impact on our estimates are Starship’s per-kg launch cost once it is reusable, which determines how quickly non-compute capex is recovered, and compute per watt for each GPU generation, which determines how much compute the same 175 kW delivers.

The Compute Payload Is Locked: A Space Version of NVL72, Codename Kessel

On August 4, SpaceX announced it is co-designing AI1’s compute payload with NVIDIA, using Vera CPUs and Rubin GPUs, with prototype testing in early 2027 and volume production later in 2027. NVIDIA’s August 24 press release described it as an optimized Vera Rubin NVL72. Asked why only NVIDIA, Musk answered, “they are the best.” NVIDIA is already scheduling production for this 2027 product; Rubin is the mainstream 2027 platform in any case, so the orbital and terrestrial versions use the same chip generation.

Choosing the highest-end parts also makes sense: launch cost scales with mass, so the more compute per ton the better, which means the satellites will carry the top-end GPUs of their generation.

Architecturally, SpaceX is following the path we laid out in June, small units in a large constellation: clusters of ~10 satellites with SpaceX-disclosed intra-cluster bandwidth of ~10 Tbps (~1.25 TB/s; SpaceX did not specify whether this is per link or the cluster aggregate); even on a per-link basis, that is only on the order of a single GPU’s NVLink bandwidth. At that level, inter-satellite links suit low-communication workloads such as inference.

We continue to expect the first generation to focus on inference completed within a single satellite and latency-insensitive batch jobs, with large-model training staying on the ground. Google’s 81-satellite Suncatcher cluster is similar in form, with small satellites in tight formation networked by inter-satellite lasers, but Google’s research targets high-bandwidth inter-satellite links for distributed machine learning (the paper’s per-link target is ~10 Tbps), so the two workloads may differ.

the space NVL72 rack architecture was finalized in October under the internal codename Kessel. It keeps the terrestrial configuration of 18 compute trays plus 9 NVLink switch trays, with all 72 GPUs still in a single NVLink domain. Structurally, the 48U upright terrestrial rack (~2.1 m tall) is compressed into a ~9U flat enclosure with a front face ~430 mm wide and 390 mm tall: a midplane in the center with a column of trays on each side.

The left column has four compute trays at the top and four at the bottom, with five switch trays in between; the right column has five compute trays at the top and five at the bottom, with four switch trays in between. Compute trays are ~25 mm thick, more than 40% thinner than a terrestrial 1U tray. The enclosure is squat, with a low center of gravity to withstand vertical launch, and its weight is cut to a few hundred kilograms.

The board design is heavily simplified: all chips are soldered directly to the board; debug interfaces and nonessential peripherals are removed, with debugging and testing completed on the ground before launch; there is no complex management network, and BlueField is dropped. On networking, NVLink connects GPUs only within the rack. From the CPU’s perspective there are 18 nodes, linked via CX9 NICs and a single top-of-rack switch, and there is no NVLink between satellites.

Cooling uses a single liquid loop: cold plates sit directly on the chips, and coolant circulates straight to the external radiator. In the 3D view, cold plates with flow channels sit on top of the trays, and the two columns of trays plug into the central midplane from either side.

Before entering eclipse, a satellite migrates the current job state to other satellites over the laser links and runs on battery at low power while in shadow, so this half of the fleet can serve inference as well.

The space version faces four main technical challenges: memory bit flips, latch-up, accelerated aging under heat and radiation, and vibration and g-loads during launch. the engineering approach is fairly clear: standard Rubin GPUs and HBM, with no switch to LPDDR and no space-specific packaging; external shielding on the satellite, extensive high-energy particle testing on the ground and additional stress testing of DRAM; HBM’s built-in ECC plus memory scrubbing in operation; and, if needed, running the memory interface one speed grade lower for margin.

Latch-up protection (overcurrent detection, power cut-off and recovery) still has to be verified, and launch loads are handled by the squat, low-center-of-gravity rack. All of this still needs on-orbit validation by the test satellites, and some in the supply chain worry that launch vibration could damage board connectors and that early satellites cannot be repaired if they fail.

Radiation tolerance and heat rejection are the two core engineering problems for the space version. The chips are standard parts, so apart from HBM’s built-in ECC, the work falls on external shielding, thermal hardware and system-level fault tolerance, concentrating demand for advanced materials and alloys in the shielding, radiator and structural components.

For radiation protection, the shield needs composite metal materials to cut total dose and low-energy particle effects within a tight mass budget, with local reinforcement around sensitive parts such as HBM. High-energy heavy ions cannot be stopped this way, so ECC, scrubbing and latch-up protection have to handle them at the system level; latch-up protection still has to be verified on the test satellite.

For thermal management, the cold plates, fluid loop and external radiator have to support high-temperature operation continuously for five years, and the non-SSO half of the fleet also goes through ~5,500 thermal cycles a year. The radiator relies on high-emissivity coatings to hold its rejection capacity (we assume an emissivity of 0.95), so coating degradation eats directly into power margin.

Structurally, a flat enclosure weighing a few hundred kilograms has to survive the loads and vibration of a vertical launch while keeping each satellite under 2 t, which calls for lightweight, high-strength alloys and composites. For materials suppliers, this is a new category of demand: volumes grow with the satellite count, components must meet aerospace requirements, and both unit value and technical barriers are higher than for terrestrial rack sheet metal.

Beyond SpaceX’s full-rack, one-rack-per-satellite form factor, on-orbit compute is also coming in a smaller form. a standardized small chassis is being offered to other satellite operators: up to 4 compute nodes and ~16 GPUs, with no custom design required, suited to lower-power satellites. Several operators plan to launch in 2027, and some may move ahead of SpaceX.

Publicly, Starcloud put an H100 into orbit in November 2025 and filed with the FCC in March for 88,000 satellites; Axiom Space and Kepler both deployed on-orbit compute nodes in January; Blue Origin filed with the FCC in March for Project Sunrise, a constellation of more than 50,000 satellites; and Cowboy Space filed in May for Stampede, a 20,000-satellite constellation, with launches planned from 2028. Both form factors will upgrade with each GPU generation, and SpaceX is no longer the only buyer of on-orbit compute.

Supply-Chain Orders Are in

SpaceX has ordered 6,000 space-specific compute racks for 2027 from a leading server ODM, which is the sole JDM on the program and is responsible for rack design and manufacturing. The racks are custom-designed to fit Starship’s payload fairing and are smaller than terrestrial racks. The 6,000 racks equate to 432,000 GPUs.

At terrestrial rack pricing (~$7.0–7.5 million per rack, per our checks), the hardware value is ~$42–45 billion, though the actual contract value and the revenue the ODM recognizes will depend on the pricing structure and delivery schedule. Lifetime volume for the program is ~30,000 racks, matching the plan for a first phase of ~30,000 satellites. At 72 GPUs per rack, that is ~2.16 million GPUs, consistent with the roughly 2 million GPUs SpaceX has indicated it will need from NVIDIA.

May is the key month: the ODM completes server NPI and builds test racks, just as Starship begins launching test satellites. This is consistent with public guidance of a prototype in early 2027, volume production later in 2027 and thousands of satellites deploying from late 2027, and Musk said on August 25 that launches could begin as early as 4Q27 and reach scale in 2028.

Once the racks are delivered, launch becomes the gating factor. All GW figures in this report refer to average compute-payload power at 175 kW per satellite, SpaceX’s stated average, with no further deduction for eclipse; that is, we treat 175 kW as the average compute-payload power over the full orbital period, pending validation by the test satellite. One AI1 carries one NVL72, so 6,000 racks at one rack per satellite equal 1.05 GW (1.5 GW peak).

At ~2 t per satellite, plus deployment hardware for an effective 2.5 t of launch capacity each, a 100 t Starship carries 40 satellites, or 7 MW per launch. That means ~143 launches per GW, consistent with our June estimate of 70 kW per ton. Launching all 6,000 satellites takes 150 dedicated launches; at the 4,000 kg filed cap, again adding deployment hardware, or 22 satellites per launch, it takes 273. The 6,000 racks are produced by December 2027, awaiting integration and launch, and form the 2028 launch pipeline. The 30,000 racks are a cumulative lifetime target: 5.25 GW (7.5 GW peak) and 750–1,360 launches in total; net of the first 6,000, the remaining 24,000 racks (~4.2 GW, 600–1,090 launches) are the increment from 2029 onward.

The Launch Schedule Is Taking Shape

The launch schedule is what will set the 2027 cadence. Starship V3 has flown three times this year, roughly two months apart, each flight going further than the last: Flight 13 in July deployed the first batch of Starlink V3 satellites, and Flight 14 in September was the first true orbital flight and Starship’s first revenue mission. To date, the ship has always splashed down at sea; recovery is the next step. Flight 15, expected no earlier than late November, will attempt to catch both the booster and the ship with the launch tower.

On the October 20 call, Musk put the odds of success at “more likely than not.” Shotwell said the license will cover return to launch site and tower catch for both stages; the plan is to bring both stages back to the tower by year-end, refly the upper stage in early 2027 and attempt on-orbit propellant transfer, and begin flying Starmind satellites in 2027. Flight 16 may be the first flight from Florida’s LC-39A.

The S-1’s 100 t fully reusable payload, multiple launches per day after tower catch and 99% reduction in cost to orbit all assume the ship comes back intact, which makes Flight 15 the most critical step in the entire timeline.

How many times can Starship fly in 2027? Company guidance is to reach one launch per week at some point in 1H27 and two per week by year-end, which implies ~50– 100 launches for the year. We do not see regulatory approval itself as the main constraint: Starbase’s expansion from 5 to 25 launches a year and LC-39A’s environmental review for 44 a year were both approved.

At SLC-37, the Air Force’s 2025 environmental review covers 76 launches a year, but the FAA only began a supplemental review in late September, and SpaceX still needs a license modification. Starbase’s current license allows 25 launches a year; Pad 2 is in use, and Pad 1 is being refurbished with a target return in 2027. The October application to the FAA for 456 launches a year (100 in phase one) is a Programmatic EIS, with scoping meetings not starting until mid-November.

Based on the 12–18-month pace of the previous two reviews, Starbase will most likely still operate at 25 in 2027. In Florida, LC-39A’s main construction is complete and its environmental review cleared 44 launches a year in January; the license is pending, and the tower is still in acceptance testing. SLC-37’s two towers have just gone up, and whether they enter service in 2027 is uncertain. The 2027 ceiling is therefore most likely Starbase’s 25 plus LC-39A’s 44, or 69 launches, with SLC-37 as upside optionality. Then there is reuse.

All three flights this year were two months apart, with hardware changes between flights; getting from one launch every two months to one a week requires catching and reflying the ship, or else every launch needs a new ship. Our base case is 30–40 launches in 2027: 1–3 a month in 1H and 4–6 a month in 2H, which sums to 30–54. Given that turnaround times will still be unstable early in the reuse ramp, we take the low end of the range for 2H. This is consistent with our earlier view that commercial operations begin in late 2027 or 2028.

Starlink V3 is also ahead of compute satellites in the queue. At ~2 t each, V3 satellites can only fly on Starship, and Shotwell has said a few hundred are needed before users notice a difference, which she expects by mid-2027; that will take up more than a dozen launches first. Assuming the test satellite flies in May and two racks ride along on each launch from 4Q27, our base case has ~12 launches available for ride-alongs in 4Q, so 2027 Starmind deployments would be ~25–30 satellites, or 4–5 MW. If the company’s weekly cadence materializes and 4Q brings full-ship Starmind launches, the figure would be 150–400 satellites, or 25–70 MW.

After the November 3 midterms, Democrats won the Texas governorship and the lieutenant governor and attorney general races; the incoming governor has promised a moratorium on new data centers, and the TCEQ has suspended data-center-related permits since September 21 (see our November 4 AIDC note). Two key operations are based in Texas: Starship launches at Starbase and Starmind integration at Bastrop. The effects run in opposite directions.

Launch is unaffected: Starship launch licenses and environmental reviews are federal (FAA), so Starbase’s expansion from 25 to 100 launches a year is not in the state’s hands. What the state controls is TCEQ wastewater and air permits, the state-law authority to close the beach, and state incentives, which affect turnaround efficiency rather than whether Starship can fly; with the state senate still Republican, changing the law is hard.

Bastrop gains in importance: the moratorium targets terrestrial data centers, orbital compute falls outside it, and blocked ground expansion in Texas raises the priority of the orbital option inside SpaceX. We are not changing our 2027 launch count of 30– 40 or the two 2028 scenarios as a result.

2028 is the year the 6,000 racks launch in volume. We model two scenarios, a base case and a bull case, each with a defined set of assumptions. Both assume 40 satellites per 100 t launch: ~2 t per satellite, or ~2.5 t of launch capacity each including deployment hardware. The base case assumes SLC-37 completes its supplemental environmental review and obtains its license modification in 2027, giving 145 available launches in 2028, half of which go to compute satellites.

The bull case further assumes phase one of Starbase’s 456-launch review is approved in 2027, giving 220 available launches, ~70% of which go to compute satellites. That is the share needed to launch all 6,000 racks, and it would leave only ~66 launches a year for Starlink and other missions.

Both launch counts are the sum of each site’s annual cap, which implies all three sites running near their caps for the full year and achieving the required turnaround times for booster and ship reuse; the ramp from 30–40 launches in 2027 to 145 depends on the catch and upper-stage reflight succeeding in early 2027, LC-39A entering service in 1H27 and SLC-37 by year-end. If satellites approach the 4,000 kg filed cap, each launch carries only 22, and the bull case’s 154 launches deliver ~3,400 satellites, or ~0.59 GW on orbit.

The same logic applies to payload per launch: Flight 14 carried 26 V3 satellites, or ~50 t at ~2 t each, and per our checks early launches will carry ~20 satellites each. If 2028 payload per launch stays at that level, the base case yields ~1,500 satellites (0.26 GW) on orbit and the bull case ~3,100 (0.54 GW).

The same applies to the power basis: if 175 kW is only a sunlit-time average, the non-SSO half of the fleet delivers only 60/95 of that, the fleet average falls to ~143 kW, and the base case yields ~0.43 GW and the bull case ~0.86 GW. In a stress case where the catch or LC-39A slips by six months and SLC-37 does not enter service, 2028 has ~50–70 launches available, half go to compute satellites, and at 20–40 satellites per launch the fleet is ~500–1,400 satellites, or ~0.1–0.25 GW.

The ~1,000 launches and ~10 GW for 2028 that Musk cited in June work out exactly at 40 satellites per launch and 250 kW peak; on our 175 kW average basis, 1,000 launches equal ~7 GW, and either way they require the Starbase expansion and more launch sites coming online.

Constraints on the Ground Push Compute Toward Orbit

Democrats won both houses of Congress in the November 3 midterms, as well as the Texas governorship and the lieutenant governor and attorney general races. Governor-elect Hinojosa has promised to pause new data centers until the legislature puts protections in place, to require projects to show they will not raise household utility bills, and to repeal the data-center sales-tax exemption. The Texas freeze began before the election: in August the state halted ERCOT’s large-load interconnection queue for project-by-project audits, and since September 21 the TCEQ has suspended data-center-related permits. Data centers were a bipartisan voter issue in this election:

about two-thirds of Americans polled oppose a data center in their area, and at least 50 local ballot measures in 13 states dealt with data centers.

At the federal level, an outright ban is unlikely; cost allocation and disclosure rules are the more likely outcome. As we argued in our November 4 AIDC note, most terrestrial projects due in 2027 are already under construction, so our 2027 delivery forecast stands. The impact falls on capacity added from 2028, when Texas accounts for nearly 30% of new U.S. capacity: most of it will be delayed, some will move to other states, and those states may adopt similar requirements in their 2027 legislative sessions.

Orbital compute sidesteps most of these bottlenecks. It needs no land, grid connection or water, and no state or local data-center permits; its approvals are federal, with the FCC for the constellation and the FAA for launch, and the Texas freeze does not reach them. The timing lines up as well: the constraints are most acute for terrestrial capacity scheduled to come online from 2028 onward, exactly when the 6,000 racks launch in volume and the remaining 24,000 follow.

On cost, terrestrial data centers are increasingly asked to pay for grid upgrades and bring their own power, which pushes up the cost of each unit of power. Google’s paper compares orbital launch cost with a terrestrial data center’s power bill, so higher terrestrial power costs raise the launch price per kilogram that orbit can absorb, which helps narrow the cost gap between the two.

After the election, Musk posted on X: “Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now.” We are not changing the launch assumptions behind our two 2028 scenarios, but tighter terrestrial permitting strengthens the demand case behind the 30,000-rack lifetime plan, and the incentive to move compute into orbit is stronger than it was before the election.

Still to Be Proven: On-Orbit Thermal Data

No AI1 has flown yet, so the thermal numbers come from models and ground tests. Among the orbital demonstrations disclosed so far, the most direct reference is Google’s Suncatcher prototype, which reached orbit on October 1; its TPUs run for ~15 minutes at a time and then pause while the radiator sheds heat. SpaceX’s stated 175 kW average is 70% of the 250 kW peak.

Whether satellites can actually average 175 kW in orbit determines whether our GW framework holds, and converting average power into delivered compute also depends on clock speeds, utilization, inter-satellite communication and availability. With GPU junction temperatures at 80–90°C, HBM’s own operating temperature and reliability still need to be validated.

Together with the radiation environment, they will determine HBM failure rates and whether the five-year life holds; the shielding, error correction and stress testing described above are aimed squarely at this. The answers will come from the test satellite launching on Starship in May 2027, and on-orbit disclosures in 1H27 are the single most important data point in the chain.

Implications for SPCX

The implications for SPCX are twofold. On valuation, we are not changing our base case. In our SOTP, the $500 billion for the launch business plus the Starship option is unchanged, and orbital compute remains outside our revenue model for the same reason as in June: full-power thermal control and sustained operation of a single satellite have not yet been demonstrated in orbit. What has changed is that two of the four conditions we set out in June have now seen substantive progress.

The four were demonstrated thermal management at the 1 MW scale (roughly the heat load of four satellites in the current single-satellite form; the more direct threshold is sustained full-power operation of one satellite), the design that can be manufactured and deployed at scale, a defined customer workload and a quantifiable asset life. Of the four, the scalable design is now finalized and backed by a 6,000-rack order for 2027, and xAI’s own workloads provide an initial source of demand.

The five-year life is a SpaceX design target that still needs to be assessed using ground-based accelerated testing and on-orbit data, and like thermal control it waits on the test satellite.

From now to the end of 2027 there are four checkpoints, each tied to one assumption in our model: the Flight 15 catch, plus the early-2027 upper-stage reflight and onorbit propellant transfer, determine whether 2027 reaches 30–40 launches; 1H27 testsatellite thermal data determine whether the 175 kW average per satellite is achievable in orbit; SLC-37’s supplemental environmental review and license modification determine the base case’s 145 launches; and weekly cadence in 2H27 plus approval of the Starbase expansion determine the bull case’s 220 launches and 6,000 satellites.

The second concerns competition and the supply chain. The moat in this market lies mainly in launch. Google has the chips and the capital, but its prototype reached orbit on a SpaceX rideshare; Blue Origin has filed for Project Sunrise, but New Glenn lifts 45 t per launch and does not recover its upper stage; and reusable heavy-lift rockets in the class of China’s Long March 9 are still years away.

Even if orbital compute economics materialize more slowly than expected, SpaceX’s advantage in launch capability remains, but the associated launch demand could slip. In the supply chain, the new visibility is in the compute payload and the racks. NVIDIA’s Rubin NVL72 gains a first batch of 6,000 orbital units, with lifetime volume of ~30,000, and the small-chassis form factor extends that demand to other satellite operators. The ODM gains a new rack platform designed for orbital use, with 6,000 units scheduled for production in 2027.

Laser links and SpaceX’s in-house solar arrays draw on the existing Starlink supply chain. In terms of timing, ODM and GPU demand shows up in 2027 with the 6,000rack delivery, while demand for laser terminals and solar arrays tracks satellite integration, starting in 2H27 with the bulk in 2028.

Risks to Monitor

We are tracking four risks:

  1. A failed or delayed catch would push back V3 deployment and Starmind together.
  1. Test-satellite data could disprove our temperature assumptions, forcing SpaceX to either cut power or add radiator area, which would worsen per-satellite economics.
  1. The FCC has only accepted, not approved, the 1-million-satellite application, and SpaceX itself acknowledged on its October safety page that tight formation flying requires other operators to share maneuver data.
  1. The 6,000-rack order locks in 2027 deliveries; whether they all launch in 2028 depends on launch-site construction and progress on ship reuse in 2027.
